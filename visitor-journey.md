# The Creature from the Deep
## Structured Visitor Journey — Design Foundation v0.1

**Status:** Working design document  
**Foundation:** Existing concept PDF, original drawing by a four-year-old child, and the sentence: “This creature lives in the ocean.”  
**Experience promise:** A child’s drawing becomes an invitation to investigate a real ocean: first through wonder, then through evidence, and finally through care.

---

## 1. Experience thesis

The creature is not a mascot and is never presented as a newly discovered species. It is an open question, a narrator, and a visible trace of a child’s imagination. Visitors repeatedly ask:

> If this creature really lived in the ocean, what would its world need to be like?

The experience uses that question to connect imagination with scientific inquiry. Every speculative feature of the creature must be paired with a real environmental condition or biological strategy. The journey therefore moves through a clear learning cycle:

**Imagine → Observe → Test → Connect → Create → Care**

Fantasy opens the door. Evidence changes the visitor’s understanding. Personal and collective choices make that understanding meaningful.

### Design principles

1. **Preserve the hand of the child.** The orange-and-blue lines remain imperfect, expressive, and recognisable throughout the experience.
2. **Make knowledge happen.** Visitors learn through changing light, sound, movement, scale, and relationships before explanatory text names the phenomenon.
3. **Offer equal dignity to all ages.** Children receive agency rather than simplification; adults receive depth rather than supervision duty.
4. **Make cause and effect legible.** Technology may be invisible, but visitors must understand why the environment responded.
5. **Use darkness deliberately, never carelessly.** Deep-sea atmosphere must coexist with safety, orientation, sensory choice, and emotional reassurance.
6. **Reward attention, not speed.** Stillness, close looking, listening, and cooperation reveal more than frantic interaction.
7. **Distinguish evidence from speculation.** The experience always signals what is observed science, plausible inference, or imaginative invention.
8. **End with attachment and agency.** The final feeling is not guilt but a desire and ability to protect a world visitors have learned to notice.

## 2. Audiences and visit modes

The core design unit is a mixed-age social group, while the experience must also work for individual adults, school groups, and visitors with different access needs.

| Audience | Primary need | Desired agency | Risk to avoid | Design response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Families with children aged 4–7 | Immediate sensory invitation, emotional safety, simple causality | Move, point, imitate, choose, draw | Text dependence, fear of darkness, waiting | Low-height discoveries, repeated visual cues, tactile preview, short action loops, bypass routes |
| Families with children aged 8–12 | Challenge, discovery, ownership | Form hypotheses, test, collaborate, create | Interactions that feel childish or predetermined | Multiple outcomes, evidence prompts, group roles, visible consequences |
| Teenagers | Autonomy, credibility, social relevance | Explore independently, compare interpretations, influence a shared system | Forced performance, moralising tone | Optional depth, collective systems, contemporary scientific questions, no compulsory role-play |
| Adults without children | Atmosphere, intellectual substance, contemplation | Observe, listen, interpret, make meaningful choices | Feeling that the exhibition is “for children” | Adult-height interpretation, slower paths, primary evidence, acoustic and visual sophistication |
| School groups | Curriculum relevance, facilitated inquiry, manageable flow | Predict, test, discuss, document | Congestion and one-right-answer tasks | Small-group missions, distributed stations, teacher preview, post-visit resources |
| Multigenerational groups | Shared conversation without a knowledge hierarchy | Contribute different forms of attention | One person operating while others watch | Parallel roles: spotter, mover, listener, connector, recorder |
| Disabled and neurodivergent visitors | Choice, predictability, equivalent access | Control pace and sensory exposure | Darkness, overload, inaccessible controls, interaction requiring one body type | Pre-visit information, sensory map, step-free route, seated reach, captions, tactile and audio alternatives, quiet bypasses |

### Layered interpretation

Each scientific idea is expressed through three coordinated layers rather than separate “children’s” and “adult” versions:

- **Experience layer:** a bodily or sensory phenomenon available without reading.
- **Discovery layer:** one concise question and a clear explanation in plain language.
- **Depth layer:** optional evidence, uncertainty, current research, and ecological context.

No layer is labelled by age. Visitors choose their own depth.

## 3. Emotional dramaturgy

The journey is a descent in six acts. Its emotional arc alternates expansion and stillness so that intensity does not simply increase.

| Act | Emotional state | Dramatic question | Learning behaviour | Transition cue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The Discovery | Recognition, tenderness, curiosity | “What could this be?” | Notice and imagine | The drawn lines begin to move and point toward water |
| 2. The Surface | Openness, play, confidence | “Is the ocean one place?” | Compare and predict | Warm light loses colour as the path slopes or visually descends |
| 3. The Descent | Anticipation, productive uncertainty | “What changes as we go deeper?” | Sense and test | Sound becomes more spatial; the creature’s orange trace becomes a guide |
| 4. The Living Ocean | Energy, interdependence, shared agency | “Who depends on whom?” | Cooperate and connect | Activity falls away; a threshold asks for stillness |
| 5. The Deep Sea | Awe, humility, intimate encounter | “How can life exist here?” | Observe closely and infer adaptation | The creature’s complete form appears, then separates into evidence and imagination |
| 6. The Archive | Expression, belonging, care | “What world will we help make possible?” | Create, classify, contribute, commit | Visitor-made beings join a living collective ocean |

### Emotional safety rhythm

- Every dark or intense zone has a visible destination, a recognisable orange orientation line, and an optional shorter route.
- Anticipatory cues announce darkness, low frequencies, moving imagery, and enclosed passages before entry.
- The deepest moment is quiet and spacious, not frightening or jump-based.
- Humour and delight appear through animal strategies and visitor discoveries, not through cartoon treatment.
- The exit restores visual warmth and social connection before any conservation invitation.

---

## 4. Learning objectives

By the end of the visit, visitors should be able to:

1. **Describe depth as a changing habitat,** not simply “more water”: light, colour, temperature, pressure, food availability, and sound conditions change with depth.
2. **Explain adaptation as a relationship** between an organism and its environment rather than as a collection of strange features.
3. **Recognise several real deep-sea strategies,** such as bioluminescence, counterillumination, pressure-adapted bodies, large sensory organs, reduced metabolism, vertical migration, and alternative ways of finding food.
4. **Trace at least one ecological relationship** among surface productivity, sinking organic matter, migration, predation, decomposition, and deep-sea food webs.
5. **Distinguish observation, inference, and invention.** Visitors can say what evidence supports an idea and where imagination begins.
6. **Understand that the deep ocean is connected to human activity** through climate regulation, fishing, pollution, noise, extraction, and scientific research.
7. **Leave with curiosity and care,** including one credible action or question that extends beyond the museum.

### Evidence of learning inside the experience

- Visitors revise an initial prediction during the descent.
- Groups activate a system only by coordinating different roles.
- Creature-making choices are constrained by habitat conditions and produce explainable trade-offs.
- Exit reflection asks visitors to identify one thing they imagined, one thing they discovered, and one thing they now want to protect or investigate.

## 5. Spatial journey

The timing below is a design hypothesis for a **45–60 minute core visit**. It can later be scaled once venue area, capacity, and operational model are known.

### Threshold — “A line enters the water”

**Duration:** 2–4 minutes  
**Purpose:** Orient visitors before the formal sequence.

- The handwritten sentence appears without interpretive framing.
- A tactile and visual map previews darkness, sound intensity, seating, exits, and the step-free route.
- Visitors can hear or read a short child-voiced introduction.
- A “What will it feel like?” preview offers light, sound, and touch samples without spoiling discoveries.

**Key decision:** Entry should feel like crossing from the museum into a hypothesis, not walking into a themed aquarium.

### Zone 1 — The Discovery

**Duration:** 5 minutes  
**Emotional purpose:** Build trust through authenticity.  
**Scientific purpose:** Introduce observation, inference, and question-making.

The original drawing is displayed as an artefact, with its paper texture, scale, and marks intact. Nearby, selected lines detach slowly into space while a child’s voice states only the original sentence. Visitors identify what they actually see before proposing what it might mean.

**Interaction: Evidence / Idea**

- Visitors select or point to a line or feature.
- The system asks: “What can you see?” and then “What might it help the creature do?”
- Responses become two visibly different kinds of trace: **evidence** and **idea**.
- There is no correct species reveal.

**Adult depth:** How scientific illustration, specimens, remotely operated vehicles, environmental DNA, and partial observations shape deep-sea knowledge.

**Access:** High-contrast facsimile, tactile line relief, audio description, child-height and seated sightlines, transcript of the voice.

### Zone 2 — The Surface

**Duration:** 7–9 minutes  
**Emotional purpose:** Confidence, breadth, kinetic pleasure.  
**Scientific purpose:** Establish the ocean as vertically and geographically varied habitats powered largely by surface light.

A bright, reflective field shows moving surface light and multiple ocean habitats without reproducing a literal aquarium tank. The orange line briefly appears among blue currents.

**Interaction: Catch the colour**

- Visitors choose coloured objects or projected marks and carry their apparent colour into progressively filtered light.
- Reds and oranges disappear first; blues persist farther.
- A tactile/audio version maps wavelength loss to pitch or pulse without implying that sound and light behave identically.

**Interaction: Build the surface pulse**

- Separate visitors control sunlight, microscopic producers, mixing, and seasonal conditions.
- The visual system shows how energy enters a food web and how some material begins to sink.

**Core idea:** The deep sea is connected to what happens above it.

**Access:** Avoid colour-only meaning; provide named colour states, luminance contrast, tactile markers, stable handrails, and a non-flashing mode.

### Zone 3 — The Descent

**Duration:** 7–9 minutes  
**Emotional purpose:** Anticipation and embodied change.  
**Scientific purpose:** Experience how conditions transform with depth.

The path compresses perceptually while remaining physically generous. Colour, temperature impression, sound, and perceived scale change in measured stages. Depth markers always pair metres with a familiar spatial comparison, without pretending the visitor is literally at pressure.

**Interaction: Instruments of descent**

- A shared rail or set of stations lets visitors compare light, temperature, pressure, and available food at several depths.
- Each control changes one variable while a “real ocean” display shows how variables interact.
- Pressure is represented through mechanical resistance, material demonstration, or safe haptics—not pressure applied to the body.

**Interaction: Follow the trace**

- The creature’s orange line appears only when visitors slow down enough to notice it.
- This quietly teaches observation and regulates crowd pace.

**Core idea:** “Deep” is a continuum of conditions, not a single mysterious zone.

**Access:** Continuous orientation edge, no mandatory slope, resting points, captions and induction-loop-compatible audio, low-frequency alternative, clear bypass.

### Zone 4 — The Living Ocean

**Duration:** 10–12 minutes  
**Emotional purpose:** Social energy and interdependence.  
**Scientific purpose:** Reveal food webs, migration, decomposition, and ecosystem relationships.

Visitors enter a responsive field in which organisms do not merely flee from bodies. Different behaviours produce different ecological effects.

**Interaction: The vertical migration**

- One group creates a safe dark interval; another opens feeding opportunities; a third tracks predators.
- The migration succeeds only when timing and conditions align.
- The interpretation connects this daily movement to carbon transport without overstating certainty.

**Interaction: The falling feast**

- Visitors track a parcel of organic material from the surface downward.
- Different organisms transform it at different depths; no single visitor can follow every pathway.
- Groups compare what remained, what was consumed, and what reached the seafloor.

**Interaction: Stillness reveals life**

- When a local area becomes quiet and relatively still, subtle animals and signals emerge.
- A visible feedback cue explains that the system is responding to reduced motion and sound, avoiding confusion or exclusion.

**Core idea:** Survival depends on relationships and timing as much as individual anatomy.

**Access:** Equivalent activation through buttons, switches, gaze/dwell, or facilitator control; no interaction requires rapid movement, standing, hearing, or speech; quiet perimeter route preserves full content.

### Zone 5 — The Deep Sea Encounter

**Duration:** 8–10 minutes  
**Emotional purpose:** Awe, intimacy, and epistemic humility.  
**Scientific purpose:** Connect environmental constraints to biological adaptations and acknowledge limits of knowledge.

This is the quietest and most visually restrained space. Real deep-sea observations appear first as fragments: light signals, silhouettes, textures, movement patterns, and instrument readings. Only then does the child’s creature resolve at architectural scale, retaining the hand-drawn line.

**Interaction: Could it live here?**

- Visitors inspect one drawn feature at a time.
- Each feature opens three cards or media states:
  - **We observe:** a real phenomenon or organism.
  - **We infer:** a plausible function under these conditions.
  - **We imagine:** an unresolved or deliberately fictional possibility.
- Visitors assemble a habitat hypothesis rather than naming a species.

**Adaptation lenses:** seeing without sunlight; producing or detecting light; locating food; moving efficiently; tolerating pressure; reproducing at low population density; avoiding detection.

**Core idea:** The deep ocean is real, diverse, and still incompletely known. Wonder grows when uncertainty is made honest.

**Access:** Gentle light levels with adaptation threshold, audio description triggered on demand, tactile adaptation models, seating with full view, captions placed away from the darkest focal field, immediate visible exit.

### Zone 6 — The Archive of Possible Life

**Duration:** 8–12 minutes  
**Emotional purpose:** Expression, belonging, and agency.  
**Scientific purpose:** Apply habitat–adaptation relationships and consolidate the distinction between science and speculation.

This zone is a working archive, not a generic drawing station. Visitors create a life form by choosing a habitat first, then making a limited set of consequential adaptations.

**Interaction: Design for a depth**

1. Choose an environmental profile: light, pressure, temperature, food pattern, substrate, and movement of water.
2. Select or draw features for sensing, movement, feeding, defence, and reproduction.
3. Test the organism in a short simulation.
4. Revise one feature after receiving evidence-based feedback.
5. Archive the result with two labels: **based on ocean science** and **imagined by us**.

Physical drawing, tactile construction, and digital creation produce equivalent archive entries. The original creature remains the first contribution, not the standard that later creations must imitate.

**Collective outcome:** New organisms inhabit a slowly changing shared projection. Their survival is not scored competitively; ecological fit and articulated reasoning are valued.

**Access:** Paper-first option, tactile parts, switch-compatible digital controls, adjustable work surfaces, save-later code or printed take-away, no handwriting requirement.

### Epilogue — “Return to the surface”

**Duration:** 3–5 minutes  
**Purpose:** Convert awe into connection without an abrupt campaign message.

- Ambient light warms gradually.
- Visitors complete: “I imagined… / I discovered… / I want to know or protect…”
- A small number of locally and globally relevant actions are presented at individual, community, research, and policy scales.
- The final content emphasises connection: the deep ocean affects climate and life at the surface, and surface decisions reach the deep.

## 6. Interaction system

### Behavioural vocabulary

All interactions should use a small, learnable vocabulary that repeats across zones:

| Behaviour | Environmental response | Learning value |
|---|---|---|
| Approach | A trace withdraws, shifts, or becomes measurable | Presence changes observation |
| Move | Currents and groups become visible | Movement has spatial consequences |
| Become still | Shy or faint phenomena emerge | Attention can reveal more than action |
| Compare | Two depths, signals, or adaptations align | Patterns become evidence |
| Cooperate | A relationship or cycle becomes complete | Systems require multiple roles |
| Create and revise | A speculative organism meets conditions | Design becomes hypothesis testing |

### Interaction quality criteria

- The response begins within 100–300 ms for direct controls, or is intentionally delayed and clearly cued for ecological processes.
- A first-time visitor can infer what changed without staff explanation.
- The scientific relationship remains valid even when the room is crowded.
- Each installation supports observing as a legitimate form of participation.
- Failure produces useful evidence, not embarrassment.
- The system does not assign fixed adult/child roles.
- Physical, sensory, and digital alternatives lead to equivalent meaning, not necessarily identical actions.
- No personal data, facial recognition, or persistent identifiable tracking is required.

## 7. Accessibility and inclusion framework

Accessibility is part of the dramaturgy and must be specified before spatial and media concepts are locked.

### Spatial

- One continuous step-free route through every narrative act, with turning space and companion seating.
- All essential interactions operable from seated and standing positions and with limited reach or dexterity.
- Rest opportunities in every major zone; no seating relegated outside the experience.
- Tactile orientation and consistent orange edge or landmark, verified for sufficient luminance contrast.
- Unambiguous exits and re-entry points; no essential content in narrow bottlenecks.

### Sensory

- Pre-visit sensory guide with light, sound, darkness, enclosure, floor texture, and expected duration.
- A preview threshold and a quiet route that preserve narrative continuity.
- No essential information conveyed by colour, sound, projection, or vibration alone.
- Captions for all speech and meaningful sound; transcripts and induction-loop or equivalent provision.
- Audio description and tactile diagrams/models for visual content.
- Control of low-frequency effects and avoidance of unexpected peaks, strobes, or forced touch.

### Cognitive and linguistic

- Plain-language core interpretation, with optional scientific depth.
- Consistent icons and verbs across all zones.
- Short instructions that demonstrate the action before requesting it.
- Multiple major visitor languages selected from audience research; avoid metaphor where it obscures safety or scientific meaning.
- Clear distinction among observation, inference, uncertainty, and fiction.

### Social and emotional

- No interaction requires public speaking, competition, or being recorded.
- Visitors may participate alone, together, or by watching.
- Staff are trained to support access tools, darkness anxiety, school-group flow, and nonverbal participation.
- Child contributions are credited with consent and safeguarded; visitor-created content is moderated without turning the archive into surveillance.

### Validation

Co-design and test with disabled adults, disabled children and families, neurodivergent visitors, educators, and access professionals at concept, low-fidelity, and in-situ prototype stages. Compliance review alone is not sufficient.

## 8. Scientific integrity framework

### Content governance

- Establish a small advisory group spanning deep-sea ecology, biological oceanography, marine technology, climate/ocean systems, conservation, and informal learning.
- Use primary research and recognised scientific institutions for claims; record sources and review dates in a content register.
- Label uncertainty explicitly and avoid presenting one unusual organism as representative of “the deep sea.”
- Keep depth ranges, pressure, light, temperature, and species examples geographically and contextually accurate.
- Avoid common simplifications: a single linear food chain, “total darkness” at one universal depth, bioluminescence as decoration, or pressure as crushing every unprotected organism.
- Review ecological action content for efficacy, relevance, and tone; do not shift systemic responsibility entirely onto individual visitors.

### Speculation protocol

Every invented feature passes through four questions:

1. Which environmental condition does it answer?
2. Is there a real analogue, and how close is the analogy?
3. What evidence supports the inference?
4. How will the experience signal the boundary of current knowledge?

## 9. Operational assumptions to test

These are starting hypotheses, not fixed requirements:

- Core visit: 45–60 minutes; short route: 25–30 minutes.
- Grouping: mostly 2–6 people, with school cohorts distributed into smaller teams.
- Experience rhythm: no more than two high-energy interactive zones consecutively.
- Capacity is set by the Living Ocean and Archive zones, not by corridor throughput.
- Major interactions should reset gracefully without a visible “game over” state.
- A facilitated version and a fully self-guided version share the same spatial narrative.
- The original drawing is physically displayed only if conservation, ownership, and consent allow; otherwise a materially faithful facsimile is used.

### Initial success measures

| Dimension | Observable indicator |
|---|---|
| Emotional | Visitors show and describe curiosity, awe, and care rather than fear or spectacle alone |
| Social | Mixed-age groups exchange observations and make at least one decision together |
| Learning | Visitors connect one adaptation to one environmental condition and revise one idea |
| Inclusion | Visitors can complete the narrative via multiple sensory and physical modes without staff rescue |
| Engagement | Dwell time is distributed across contemplation, interaction, and interpretation rather than concentrated at screens |
| Integrity | Visitors can distinguish the child’s imagined creature from real organisms and evidence |

## 10. Next concrete design decisions

### Decision gate 1 — Define the frame

Resolve before detailed spatial design:

1. Venue, usable footprint, ceiling height, entry/exit conditions, and whether the experience is permanent, touring, or temporary.
2. Target throughput, peak group size, school-group strategy, average and maximum dwell time.
3. Capital and operating budget ranges, staffing model, maintenance capacity, and expected lifespan.
4. Languages, regional audience profile, safeguarding requirements, and ownership/permission for the child’s drawing and voice.
5. Accessibility standard, co-design partners, evacuation constraints, and sensory operating limits.

### Decision gate 2 — Lock the narrative architecture

1. Confirm the six-act sequence and whether visitors may branch or must descend linearly.
2. Select 4–6 scientific anchor phenomena; recommended initial set:
   - loss of light and colour with depth;
   - pressure and material adaptation;
   - bioluminescence and sensing;
   - diel vertical migration;
   - marine snow and deep food webs;
   - human–deep-ocean connection.
3. Define the final status of the creature: persistent mystery, plausible composite, or explicitly evolving hypothesis. **Recommendation: persistent hypothesis.**
4. Decide how much of the original child’s voice and authorship appears, with consent and without manufacturing additional quotations.

### Decision gate 3 — Prototype the experience’s DNA

Build and test these three prototypes before producing full scenic concepts:

1. **Stillness reveals life** — test whether visitors understand the delayed response, whether it works in groups, and whether access alternatives preserve meaning.
2. **Could it live here?** — test the evidence/inference/imagination framework with children and adults; measure whether it strengthens rather than collapses wonder.
3. **Design for a depth** — test whether habitat-first constraints produce meaningful creativity without becoming a quiz or character creator.

For each prototype, test comprehension, emotional tone, mixed-age collaboration, accessibility, throughput, reset behaviour, and facilitator burden.

### Decision gate 4 — Establish the sensory score

1. Produce one cross-zone storyboard showing light level, colour, sound density, spatial openness, interaction intensity, and emotional state.
2. Define the orange line’s exact narrative role and frequency so it remains a precious trace rather than a navigation gimmick.
3. Create material samples that feel oceanic through translucency, reflection, scale, and movement rather than literal rockwork or aquarium imitation.
4. Commission an early spatial sound sketch with a true quiet state and an accessible alternative mix.

### Decision gate 5 — Build the evidence base

1. Create a claim-and-source register for all scientific statements and interaction models.
2. Recruit scientific, learning, accessibility, and child/family advisors.
3. Conduct front-end research with the priority audience groups to understand existing concepts of the deep ocean, sources of fear, and expectations of participation.
4. Define evaluation questions and baseline measures before design development.

## 11. Recommended immediate work package

The next design sprint should produce:

1. A scaled adjacency diagram for the six zones plus threshold and epilogue.
2. A one-page experience score mapping emotion, science, interaction, sound, light, and access across the full journey.
3. Three low-fidelity core-interaction prototypes.
4. A content matrix linking every zone to learning objective, evidence, interpretive layer, and access mode.
5. A visual language board derived from the original drawing: line, colour, motion, typography, material, and image policy.
6. A decision log recording confirmed constraints, open questions, owners, and dates.

The first irreversible design work should wait until Decision gate 1 is resolved. The three prototypes, front-end audience research, and scientific source register can begin immediately because they will reduce risk under almost any venue scenario.

---

## 12. Working north star

> Visitors enter because they want to meet an impossible creature. They leave having encountered a real ocean that is stranger, more connected, and more worthy of care than they imagined.
